\begin{abstract}
Backpropagation (\BP{}) remains the dominant algorithm for training deep
neural networks, yet its memory and energy costs motivate the search for
practical alternatives. We present a hardware-validated benchmarking
study of three forward-only, BP-free training algorithms --- Forward-Forward
(\FF{}), Cascaded Forward (\CaFo{}) and Mono-Forward (\MF{}) --- against
tuned \BP{} baselines on MLP and 3-block CNN architectures across four
standard vision benchmarks, with energy, time and memory read directly from
the device through the NVIDIA Management Library (NVML). Two results follow
from enforcing a single stopping rule across algorithms. First, \MF{} matches
or surpasses \BP{} in accuracy on its native MLP architectures, by
\numrange{0.01}{5.04}~pp, and holds up to \SI{27}{\percent} less per-process
device memory; but it is not cheaper to train. An earlier reading of these
experiments reported \SI{41}{\percent} less energy and \SI{34}{\percent} less
time for \MF{}; that comparison charged the two algorithms different amounts
of training before either could stop, and equalising the rule reverses its
sign. Second, an ablation ladder that interpolates between \BP{} and \MF{}
one change at a time --- adding a deep-supervision \BP{} baseline, a learned
readout, gradient locality and activation caching --- attributes essentially
the entire cost increase to gradient locality (\numrange{228}{272}~pp of
\BP{}'s energy) and shows that deep supervision alone reproduces \MF{}'s
accuracy on the MNIST-scale tasks but not on CIFAR, where \MF{} leads it by
over \num{5}~pp. All measurements, code and per-run traces are public.
\end{abstract}

backpropagation-free learning \and forward-only algorithms \and energy

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Deep neural networks have achieved transformative success across
AI~\cite{krizhevsky2012imagenet}, yet this
progress rests on an escalating energy budget; by 2030 the operational
requirements of such models are projected to consume between 3\% and 4\% of
global electricity~\cite{wef2025paradox}. Individual training runs are already
substantial: \cite{strubell2019energy} estimated roughly
\SI{284}{\tonne} of CO$_2$e for a single large Transformer trained with
neural architecture search, and \cite{patterson2021carbon} reported
approximately \SI{552}{\tonne} for GPT-3.

Among the algorithmic contributors to that cost,
backpropagation (\BP{})~\cite{rumelhart1986learning} is central. Its
dependence on a full backward pass requires storing all intermediate
activations, giving memory costs proportional to network depth, and its
layer-locked structure imposes a ``backward locking'' bottleneck that
constrains parallelisation~\cite{jaderberg2017decoupled}. This motivates
alternatives built on local learning rules and layer-by-layer credit
assignment. None of the algorithms studied here claims full biological
plausibility~\cite{lillicrap2020backpropagation}; we treat that lineage as
motivating context rather than as a validated claim. We study three such
algorithms: \textbf{Forward-Forward}
(\FF{})~\cite{hinton2022forward}, which learns from a contrastive
``goodness'' objective over positive and negative forward passes;
\textbf{Cascaded Forward} (\CaFo{})~\cite{zhao2023cafo}, which attaches a
local classifier to each of a cascade of neural blocks; and
\textbf{Mono-Forward} (\MF{})~\cite{gong2025mono}, which gives every hidden
layer a learnable projection matrix and a local cross-entropy loss.

A fair evaluation of these methods against \BP{} on direct hardware-level
metrics remains largely unexplored; existing comparisons often differ in
architecture or tuning effort, which confounds the
algorithm~\cite{desislavov2024energy}. This paper closes that gap, and in
doing so reaches a conclusion the earlier literature --- including an
earlier reading of our own experiments --- did not: once the stopping rule
is the same for every algorithm, none of the three forward-only methods is
cheaper to train than \BP{}. The interesting question is then \emph{why},
which we answer by ablation rather than by attribution to the algorithm as
a whole.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We contribute \emph{(i)}~a comparative benchmarking framework mandating
identical native architectures, an equal tuning budget for every method, a
single stopping rule and direct hardware-level measurement via NVML, with
every protocol asymmetry we found in our own earlier configuration disclosed
and corrected (Section~\ref{sec:framework}); \emph{(ii)}~a hardware-validated
benchmark of \MF{} against a tuned \BP{} baseline, which matches or surpasses
\BP{} in accuracy on every tested MLP and holds materially less per-process
device memory, but costs more time and energy under an equal stopping rule;
\emph{(iii)}~an ablation ladder from \BP{} to \MF{} --- deep supervision,
learned readout, gradient locality, activation caching --- that attributes the
cost to gradient locality alone and separates \MF{}'s accuracy advantage from
the effect of localised objectives (Section~\ref{sec:ladder}); and
\emph{(iv)}~an empirical re-evaluation of memory efficiency, showing that
algorithm-specific overhead can offset the theoretical saving from omitting
the backward pass, and that the device-wide memory reading commonly quoted is
dominated by the CUDA context.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:algorithms}
% ============================================================

We state each algorithm's update mechanism, because that mechanism --- not the
parameter count --- determines the shape of the resulting GPU workload.
\textbf{Forward-Forward} (\FF{})~\cite{hinton2022forward} replaces the
forward--backward pair with two forward passes, on ``positive'' (real) and
``negative'' (contrastive) data; each layer locally maximises a ``goodness''
score on the former and minimises it on the latter, so credit assignment never
leaves the layer. This doubles the data volume per step and supervises only
indirectly, the usual explanation for its slow
convergence~\cite{hinton2022forward,zhao2023cafo}. \textbf{Cascaded Forward}
(\CaFo{})~\cite{zhao2023cafo} follows each of a cascade of blocks with a local
auxiliary classifier, giving direct cross-entropy supervision at several
depths and removing the need for negative samples; blocks are either randomly
initialised and frozen (\CaFoRand{}) or pretrained with Direct Feedback
Alignment (\CaFoDFA{}), which pays an extra pretraining stage before any
predictor is useful. \textbf{Mono-Forward} (\MF{})~\cite{gong2025mono} equips
each hidden layer with a learnable projection matrix $M_i$ mapping activations
to class-wise goodness scores, on which a local cross-entropy loss is
computed; layer $i$ and $M_i$ are updated from that loss alone with the
incoming activation \emph{detached}, so no gradient crosses a layer boundary
and the layers train in sequence. That detachment is the property
Section~\ref{sec:ladder} isolates: it is what permits activation caching, and
it is also what costs.

\paragraph{Forward-only and local-loss methods.}
\FF{} has been extended with convolutional channel-wise competitive
learning~\cite{papachristodoulou2024convolutional} and with collaborative
layer objectives~\cite{lorberbom2024layer}; both report accuracy gains over
baseline \FF{} but no hardware-level efficiency comparison of the kind
conducted here. \cite{ren2023scaling} showed that forward-gradient methods
augmented with local auxiliary losses scale favourably, grounding \MF{}'s
local projections, and \cite{ishikawa2025local} analysed local loss
optimisation in the infinite-width limit, showing that greedy layer-wise
training can reduce the effective complexity of the learned representation ---
the basis of the regularisation hypothesis of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion_why_mf}.

\paragraph{Deep supervision.}
Attaching auxiliary classifiers to intermediate layers and training end-to-end
is the deep-supervision construction of~\cite{lee2015deeply}. \MF{} resembles
it closely, differing only in that \MF{} detaches the incoming activation and
reads out through $M_L$. We exploit that near-identity in
Section~\ref{sec:ladder}: a deep-supervision \BP{} baseline is the control
that separates the effect of localised objectives from the effect of
forward-only training.

\paragraph{Hardware and energy.}
GPUs and TPUs~\cite{jouppi2017datacenter} are designed around \BP{}'s
forward--backward structure, and \cite{strubell2019energy}
and~\cite{patterson2021carbon} give the empirical grounding for its energy
cost. Our contribution is evaluative rather than algorithmic: to our knowledge
this is the first hardware-validated comparison of \FF{}, \CaFo{} and \MF{}
under one fairness framework, with a deep-supervision control.

\section{Framework for Fair and Rigorous Comparison}
\label{sec:framework}
% ============================================================

Throughout, a \emph{hardware-validated comparison} is one in which every
efficiency claim is backed by a physical quantity read from the GPU itself
while the workload runs --- energy, elapsed time and memory occupancy ---
rather than by an analytical proxy such as a FLOP or parameter count.

\paragraph{Fairness Protocols.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Native architectures.} Each algorithm runs on the
        architecture of its source publication: \FF{} and \MF{} on their
        MLPs, \CaFo{} on its 3-block CNN\@. CIFAR inputs are flattened to
        1-D vectors for MLP experiments, following the \MF{}
        protocol~\cite{gong2025mono}.
  \item \textbf{Identical \BP{} baselines.} Each \BP{} baseline is built
        from the same module list as its forward-only counterpart with the
        algorithm-specific components removed --- goodness heads for \FF{},
        projection matrices for \MF{}, block predictors for \CaFo{}. A
        reviewer observed that this removal could itself account for the
        differences we report. It can, and Section~\ref{sec:ladder} measures
        how much, by putting those components back one at a time on top of
        \BP{}.
  \item \textbf{Equal trial budget, algorithm-appropriate search spaces.}
        Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna} tuned every algorithm and every \BP{}
        baseline for every configuration under an identical trial budget and
        trial epoch cap, maximising validation accuracy. The spaces are not
        identical, because the algorithms do not share a parameter set, but
        each covers at least three decades of learning rate and weight decay.
        The spaces and selected values are in the repository.
  \item \textbf{One stopping rule.} All algorithms stop on validation
        loss~\cite{prechelt1998early} with the same minimum improvement,
        patience and epoch cap (Table~\ref{tab:early_stopping}).
  \item \textbf{Fixed random seeds.} The submitted version used three seeds
        (42, 123, 7). Every result here is re-measured over at least five,
        and the \BP{}$\to$\MF{} ladder over twenty or more; seed counts are
        stated per row and per caption.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Disclosure: what the submitted version actually did.}
An audit of our own configuration files against the protocols above found
three asymmetries, none of which any reviewer raised and all of which we
correct here.
\emph{(i)~The stopping rule was not consistent}
(Table~\ref{tab:early_stopping}). \BP{} stopped on validation loss, \FF{} on
validation \emph{accuracy} with a \num{0.01} minimum improvement, and \CaFo{}
and \MF{} on per-predictor and per-layer local losses with per-configuration
patiences and caps. Because those caps apply per stage rather than per run, a
nominal cap of 15 epochs on a four-stage \MF{} network permits 60 epochs of
training. Time and energy are integrals over training duration, so an
inconsistent stopping rule does not perturb the efficiency comparison --- it
determines it. This is the single largest correction in this revision.
\emph{(ii)~Pruning was asymmetric.} \BP{}'s Optuna studies used a median
pruner while the others ran unpruned, so \BP{}'s nominal 50 trials included
truncated ones and its effective search was the weaker. The asymmetry
therefore \emph{disfavoured} \BP{}, i.e.\ it worked in the direction of the
submitted paper's conclusion. Pruning is now disabled for every algorithm,
paid for by a uniform \num{20}-epoch trial cap.
\emph{(iii)~Three configurations were not independently searched.} The Optuna
sampler was seeded from the global experiment seed, so two studies over the
same space returned byte-identical values: the \BP{} MNIST 4$\times$2000
baseline shared its hyperparameters with \BP{} MNIST 3$\times$1000, and both
\CaFo{} CIFAR-100 variants with their CIFAR-10 counterparts. The sampler seed
is now derived from the study name, and all three were re-searched; the
replacements differ by \num{2.9}$\times$, \num{2.2}$\times$ and
\num{15.3}$\times$ in at least one value.

\paragraph{Architecture, optimiser and implementation.}
All weights and projection matrices use Kaiming uniform initialisation.
\BP{} and \texttt{BP-DS} use AdamW; \CaFo{} predictors and \MF{} layers use
Adam on a local cross-entropy loss. The MLPs are fully connected with ReLU,
2--4 hidden layers of 1000 or 2000 units. Each \CaFo{} block is a
$3{\times}3$ convolution, ReLU, $2{\times}2$ max-pooling and batch
normalisation with 32, 128 and 512 output channels; the three block
predictors are summed to form the prediction. \FF{} uses a fixed goodness
threshold $\theta=2.0$ with peer normalisation (factor 0.03, momentum 0.9).
Every algorithm is implemented in the same PyTorch codebase on the same
\texttt{nn.Linear} and \texttt{nn.Conv2d} primitives, differing only in the
loss construction and in where \texttt{detach()} is called, so no method
benefits from a fused or hand-written kernel the others lack.

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
Experiments used MNIST~\cite{lecun1998gradient},
Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion}, CIFAR-10 and
CIFAR-100~\cite{krizhevsky2009learning} on NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB GPUs
(Athena cluster, ACK Cyfronet AGH) hosted on AMD EPYC 7742 nodes, with
driver 595.71.05, Python~3.10.4 and PyTorch~2.4.0+cu121. The driver version
is stated because it governs NVML power-reading semantics. For CIFAR-10 and
CIFAR-100, random horizontal flips ($p{=}0.5$) and random $32{\times}32$
crops with 4-pixel padding were applied identically to every algorithm
\emph{and} its \BP{} baseline, before flattening; MNIST and Fashion-MNIST
were not augmented. The full environment listing, the Optuna search spaces
and the exact NVML API calls are in the repository
(Section~\ref{sec:repository}).

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics and instruments.}
Accuracy is measured on the held-out test split. Efficiency is measured
directly, and we name the instrument for every quantity because they are not
interchangeable. A monitor thread queries the NVIDIA Management Library
(NVML) --- the driver's own telemetry interface, chosen because it reports
physical device state rather than an analytical proxy --- at \SI{5}{\hertz}:
power
from \texttt{nvmlDeviceGetPowerUsage}, from which energy (\si{\watt\hour}) is
the trapezoidal integral over the run; utilisation from
\texttt{nvmlDeviceGetUtilizationRates}; SM clock from
\texttt{nvmlDeviceGetClockInfo}; device memory from
\texttt{nvmlDeviceGetMemoryInfo}; and wall-clock time from the host monotonic
clock. Two memory figures exist and must not be conflated: the NVML reading is
\emph{device-wide} and includes a fixed ${\approx}\SI{800}{\mebibyte}$ CUDA
context that no algorithm controls, whereas
\texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated} is the per-process allocator
high-water mark. We report the per-process figure and say so. Host resident set
size, where quoted, comes from \texttt{psutil} and is a CPU-side quantity.
Estimated CO$_2$e and GFLOPs are collected but reported only in the repository.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ============================================================

Under a stopping rule applied uniformly to every algorithm, none of the three
forward-only methods is cheaper than \BP{}; what separates them is how they
spend the extra cost and what they buy with it. Results are mean~$\pm$~SD over
the seed counts stated per row.

% ---- 5.1 ---------------------------------------------------
\needspace{3\baselineskip}
\subsection{\FF{}: A Conceptually Sound but Computationally Costly
Starting Point}

{\looseness=-1
\FF{} trains to accuracy comparable with \BP{} on its native MLP
architectures, which validates its core premise, but it does so at a
prohibitive efficiency cost (Table~\ref{tab:headline_summary}). On the
Fashion-MNIST 4$\times$2000 MLP it takes \num{7.4}$\times$ longer and
draws \num{6.3}$\times$ the energy of the \BP{} baseline; on MNIST
4$\times$2000 the corresponding factors are \num{6.5}$\times$ and
\num{5.9}$\times$. The accuracy advantage claimed in the submitted
version does not survive re-measurement. The submitted \FF{} numbers were
produced by a checkpoint-selection defect that restored an epoch-2
network whenever the stopping metric was a loss rather than an accuracy;
with that corrected and seven seeds per arm, \FF{} is \num{0.70}~pp
\emph{below} \BP{} on Fashion-MNIST
($\text{CI}=[-\num{0.87},-\num{0.54}]$) and indistinguishable from it on
MNIST ($-\num{0.06}$~pp, $[-\num{0.21},+\num{0.07}]$). The efficiency
penalty is therefore not purchased by an accuracy margin; there is no
margin.
\par}

\input{tables/generated/headline_summary}

The cost is not a matter of power draw: \FF{} and \BP{} sit at comparable
wattage, and \FF{}'s energy bill is run up almost entirely by how long it
takes to reach its stopping criterion. Decomposing the end-to-end gap into
epochs and cost per epoch makes this exact. \FF{} needs
\numrange{2.7}{3.8}$\times$ more epochs than \BP{} but spends only
\numrange{1.5}{2.0}$\times$ as much time and
\numrange{1.5}{1.8}$\times$ as much energy \emph{per epoch}. Its disadvantage
is convergence speed, not per-step inefficiency. The NVML traces rule out the
obvious alternative explanations. Both algorithms hold the SM clock near its
ceiling throughout (\SI{1323}{\mega\hertz} for \BP{},
\SI{1274}{\mega\hertz} for \FF{}), so the penalty is not throttling; \FF{}'s
within-run clock variability is larger (SD \SI{114}{\mega\hertz} against
\SI{67}{\mega\hertz}) but far too small to account for a \num{7.4}$\times$
gap. And on both instruments---NVML device-wide
(\SI{296}{\mebibyte} against \SI{287}{\mebibyte}) and the \texttt{torch}
allocator---\FF{} consumed slightly \emph{more} peak memory than \BP{},
refuting a key presumed advantage.


% ---- 5.2 ---------------------------------------------------
\needspace{3\baselineskip}
\subsection{\CaFo{}: A Structured Alternative with Acute Trade-offs}

\CaFo{}, on its native 3-block CNN, shows a stark accuracy--efficiency
trade-off contingent on its feature-learning strategy---but neither variant is
efficient. Re-measured with five seeds, \CaFoRand{} is \num{3.7}$\times$
slower and draws \num{3.2}$\times$ the energy of \BP{} on CIFAR-10 while
losing \num{18.8}~pp of accuracy, and \CaFoDFA{} recovers much of that
accuracy only by spending \num{7.9}$\times$ the time and \num{8.3}$\times$ the
energy (Table~\ref{tab:headline_summary}). Across all four datasets the
time penalty ranges from \num{3.6}$\times$ to \num{10.5}$\times$ and the
energy penalty from \num{2.9}$\times$ to \num{10.9}$\times$. The one genuine
win is memory: \CaFoRand{} holds \num{16}\% less peak per-process device
memory than \BP{} on CIFAR-10, because its fixed random extractors need no
backward pass. As with \FF{}, the per-epoch decomposition is more flattering
than the total: \CaFoRand{} is \emph{cheaper} than \BP{} per data pass
(\numrange{0.76}{0.90}$\times$ the energy per epoch) and pays for it with
\numrange{3.6}{7.8}$\times$ more passes, whereas \CaFoDFA{} on CIFAR is
expensive on both axes. These figures replace the back-ported values of the
submitted version, which were measured on an earlier software stack and
overstated \CaFo{}'s standing considerably.


The \CaFoDFA{} power trace opens with an elevated
${\approx}\SI{125}{\watt}$ plateau: the DFA block-pretraining stage, during
which no predictor is yet useful for the downstream task. That cost is
included in every reported \CaFoDFA{} time and energy figure. The design
insight is that high-quality features are necessary for accuracy but that DFA
is not an affordable way to obtain them.

% ---- 5.3 ---------------------------------------------------
\needspace{3\baselineskip}
\subsection{\MF{}: Higher Accuracy and Lower Memory, at a Higher Total
Cost}

Mono-Forward matches or surpasses \BP{} accuracy on every tested MLP
architecture, and the margin widens with task difficulty: $+\num{0.19}$~pp
on MNIST, $+\num{0.01}$~pp on Fashion-MNIST, $+\num{3.90}$~pp on CIFAR-10
and $+\num{5.04}$~pp on CIFAR-100, each over twenty seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:headline_summary}). It also holds materially less
per-process device memory on the wider models---\num{27}\% less on
CIFAR-10 and \num{25}\% less on CIFAR-100---which is the structural
consequence of never storing a global activation graph.
Note that CIFAR-10 accuracy for MLP architectures is substantially lower
than CNN-based state-of-the-art owing to the elimination of spatial
structure via flattening; the comparison here is strictly between an
MLP-based \MF{} and an MLP-based \BP{} baseline on identical architectures.

What does not hold is the efficiency claim. Under the harmonised rule \MF{} is
\num{4.0}$\times$ slower than \BP{} and draws \num{3.2}$\times$ the energy on
CIFAR-10, with the same pattern on all four configurations. The submitted
version reported the opposite --- \MF{} \num{33.81}\% faster and \num{40.78}\%
less energy-hungry --- and that result is reproducible, but only under the
legacy protocol, in which \MF{}'s patience is spent once per layer while
\BP{}'s is spent once for the whole network. Re-running it on the current
cluster over three seeds recovers \num{33.6}\% $[\num{30.2},\num{36.0}]$ and
\num{42.4}\% $[\num{38.9},\num{44.8}]$, so the discrepancy is not a
measurement error; it is that the comparison charged the two algorithms
different amounts of training before either was allowed to stop. Equalising
the rule reverses the sign. Section~\ref{sec:ladder} isolates which
architectural choice is responsible.

Hardware monitoring on the CIFAR-10 task shows what \MF{}'s local training
regime actually does to the device
(Figure~\ref{fig:mf_hardware_and_memory}). \MF{} draws less power and keeps
the GPU less than half as busy as \BP{} (\SI{7.3}{\percent} against
\SI{16.2}{\percent} mean utilisation), but it stays resident almost four times
as long, so the lower instantaneous draw does not translate into lower energy.
Its per-layer stages are visible as steps in both the utilisation and the
memory trace.

Across all \num{20} seeds of every rung, total energy and training time are
collinear along a single iso-power band: the rungs differ in how long they
run, not in how hard they drive the device. \MF{} nonetheless reaches higher
accuracy than \BP{} on identical architectures; Section~\ref{sec:discussion_why_mf}
discusses a regularisation hypothesis. We do not compare validation-loss
curves, because \MF{} optimises a separate local objective per layer and logs
no global validation series.

% ---- 5.4 ---------------------------------------------------
\needspace{3\baselineskip}
\subsection{Where the Cost Comes From}
\label{sec:ladder}

Attributing \MF{}'s cost to \MF{} as a whole explains nothing. We therefore
interpolate between \BP{} and \MF{} in single-change steps---auxiliary
supervision, the learned readout $M_L$, gradient locality, and, where
activations can be cached, two caching strategies---and measure each step
separately (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder_waterfall}). One step dominates. Detaching
the gradient at every layer boundary adds between \num{228} and \num{272}~pp of
\BP{}'s energy across the four configurations, while auxiliary supervision and
the readout together move it by at most \num{40}~pp and on the two CIFAR tasks
partly cancel. The overhead is not an implementation detail that tuning will
remove: it is the price of locality itself.

\paragraph{Does locality buy anything deep supervision does not?}
Rung~1 of the ladder, \texttt{BP-DS}, is \BP{} with \MF{}'s auxiliary
classifiers attached and nothing detached; comparing it with \MF{} isolates
the effect of forward-only training with everything else held fixed. The
answer splits by task scale. On the two MNIST-scale 2$\times$1000 MLPs the two
are statistically equivalent within the $\pm\num{0.25}$~pp margin: \MF{}
leads by \num{0.11}~pp $[\num{-0.01},\num{+0.23}]$ on MNIST ($n=20$) and
trails by \num{0.06}~pp $[\num{-0.17},\num{+0.06}]$ on Fashion-MNIST ($n=48$).
On the two CIFAR 3$\times$2000 MLPs it is not close: \MF{} leads
\texttt{BP-DS} by \num{5.56}~pp $[\num{+5.37},\num{+5.74}]$ on CIFAR-10 and
\num{6.10}~pp $[\num{+5.88},\num{+6.31}]$ on CIFAR-100, both over \num{20}
seeds. Deep supervision alone is \emph{worse} than plain \BP{} on those two
tasks ($-\num{1.65}$ and $-\num{1.06}$~pp), whereas \MF{} is better, so the
detachment is contributing accuracy rather than merely being tolerated. We
report both halves: on small tasks \MF{}'s accuracy is attributable to
auxiliary supervision, and on harder ones it is not.

\paragraph{The time--memory frontier is a structural property.}
Caching activations is the obvious remedy for the recomputation that locality
forces, and it does not work. Caching on the device buys no time
(\numrange{1.00}{1.02}$\times$) and costs about \num{40}\% more per-process
memory; caching on the host is slower still (\num{0.97}$\times$) and adds
\num{261}~pp of \BP{}'s energy on MNIST and \num{441}~pp on Fashion-MNIST on
top of \MF{}'s own overhead. Neither strategy reaches a time--memory point
that \MF{} without caching does not already dominate. The frontier is not
merely an evaluation artefact, because which methods may appear on it at all
is decided by the algorithm: \BP{}, \texttt{BP-DS} and \texttt{MF-Joint}
cannot cache, since a joint gradient makes any cached activation stale within
one optimiser step, whereas \MF{} can, because detachment plus
layer-sequential training freeze every preceding layer for the whole of a
stage. The trade-off axis exists only for the local method. That it turns out
to be a bad trade is the empirical part; that it is available at all is the
algorithmic part.

\paragraph{Is this simply an unoptimised implementation?}
The natural objection is that \BP{} looks good because a decade of kernel
engineering has gone into it. We tested that rather than arguing it. \MF{}'s
layer-sequential schedule recomputes the activations of every preceding layer
at each stage, an $O(L^2)$ cost a mature implementation would remove by
caching, so we implemented the cache and checked it against the recomputing
path: over \num{1200} and \num{3600} optimiser steps the maximum absolute
difference in both loss and weights was exactly \num{0}, so the optimisation is
bit-exact. It bought no speedup (\numrange{1.00}{1.02}$\times$ on device,
\num{0.97}$\times$ on host). The missing optimisation was not missing; it
simply does not pay, because at these layer widths the device never exceeds
${\approx}\SI{38}{\percent}$ mean utilisation and the runs are bound by kernel
launch latency rather than arithmetic. The same reading covers the other two
algorithms: per data pass \CaFoRand{} is \emph{cheaper} than \BP{} and \FF{}
only \numrange{1.5}{2.0}$\times$ more expensive, so the end-to-end gaps are
made almost entirely of extra epochs. Library maturity is not what separates
these methods from \BP{}; convergence rate is.

\paragraph{Intervals, not verdicts.}
Of \num{28} accuracy contrasts, eleven are equivalent within the preregistered
margin, sixteen are different, and one is inconclusive
(Figure~\ref{fig:equivalence_forest}): \CaFoDFA{} against \BP{} on the
Fashion-MNIST CNN, whose interval spans $[\num{-0.03},\num{+0.61}]$~pp at five
seeds --- wide enough to contain both the margin and values well outside it.
We mark such a contrast inconclusive rather than as evidence of parity.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

We interpret the empirical results, discuss methodological limitations,
and draw implications for future work. Where interpretations go beyond
the data, we label them explicitly as hypotheses.

\paragraph{Why \MF{} is accurate: a regularisation hypothesis.}
\label{sec:discussion_why_mf}
\FF{}'s inefficiency traces to its indirect goodness signal and contrastive
data generation, which cost it \numrange{2.7}{3.8}$\times$ more epochs than
\BP{}; \CaFo{}'s DFA pretraining fixes the feature-quality problem at an
unaffordable price. A direct supervisory signal appears necessary, and \MF{}'s
local projections supply one with minimal overhead --- a mechanism close to
the local auxiliary losses of \cite{ren2023scaling} and
\cite{ishikawa2025local}, the latter showing in the infinite-width limit that
greedy layer-wise training can reduce the effective complexity of the learned
representation. This motivates the \emph{regularisation hypothesis}: that
greedy layer-wise optimisation implicitly constrains each layer's
representational capacity and so generalises better than joint optimisation.
The ladder sharpens it. Auxiliary supervision alone reproduces \MF{}'s
accuracy on the MNIST-scale tasks but \emph{loses} \numrange{1.06}{1.65}~pp
against plain \BP{} on the CIFAR tasks, exactly where \MF{} gains
\numrange{3.90}{5.04}~pp, so the credit belongs to the detachment rather than
to the heads, and only once the task is hard enough. It remains a hypothesis;
testing it needs diagnostics we have not run, such as the train--validation
gap over training or sharpness measures.

\paragraph{Memory efficiency depends on which instrument you read.}
\label{sec:discussion_memory}
Measured per process with \texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated}, \MF{}
holds \num{27}\% less than \BP{} on the CIFAR-10 3$\times$2000 MLP and
\num{25}\% less on CIFAR-100 --- far more than the ${\approx}\num{5}\%$ the
submitted version reported, which came from the device-wide NVML counter in
which a fixed ${\approx}\SI{800}{\mebibyte}$ CUDA context dilutes every
difference. \CaFoRand{} saves \numrange{6}{7}\% on the same basis, while
\FF{} and \CaFoDFA{} consume \emph{more} memory than their \BP{} baselines:
dual data streams, feedback tensors and projection matrices are real
allocations that offset the activation graph one avoids storing. Report the
per-process figure, and do not treat ``no backward pass'' as a memory argument
without measuring it.

\paragraph{Implications for efficient training and hardware co-design.}
\label{sec:discussion_hardware_codesign}
The efficiency case for forward-only training does not survive a harmonised
stopping rule on this platform, and the traces say why. No configuration we
measured exceeds ${\approx}\SI{38}{\percent}$ mean GPU utilisation, and \MF{}
reaches only \SI{7.3}{\percent} against \BP{}'s \SI{16.2}{\percent} on
CIFAR-10: the layer-sequential schedule issues many small kernels and waits,
so the device idles while the run stays resident and the fixed power floor is
integrated over four times the duration. This is a launch-latency problem
rather than an arithmetic one, which is also why a bit-exact activation cache
removed the $O(L^2)$ recomputation without saving any time. That diagnosis is
what makes the result more than negative: layer-local updates are exactly the
work that a pipelined or memory-local execution model need not serialise, and
the per-process memory reduction is structural. \MF{} is thus an accuracy and
memory result on current hardware and a conditional energy result on hardware
that does not yet exist; extrapolating to a sustainability claim would require
the advantage at the scale where training energy is societally
significant~\cite{strubell2019energy,patterson2021carbon}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Architecture and scale.} Results apply to 2--4-layer MLPs
        (\FF{}, \MF{}) and a 3-block CNN (\CaFo{}). Deeper CNNs, residual
        networks and transformers are untested, as is behaviour under
        increasing depth and width, though prior work on local-loss
        methods~\cite{ren2023scaling} suggests scalability.
  \item \textbf{Datasets.} The four benchmarks are well established but
        small, and the MLP experiments flatten CIFAR inputs to 1-D vectors
        following the \MF{} protocol, which destroys spatial structure and
        caps absolute accuracy far below CNN state-of-the-art. The
        comparisons are internally valid but say nothing about either
        algorithm in a competitive vision pipeline.
  \item \textbf{Hardware specificity.} Every efficiency figure comes from one
        NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB. Since our central diagnosis is that these
        workloads are launch-latency-bound rather than compute-bound, the
        results are especially likely to move on hardware with different
        kernel-launch and memory-bandwidth characteristics, so we regard the
        direction of the energy result as platform-conditional.
  \item \textbf{Statistical resolution.} Seed counts are now
        \numrange{5}{122} per contrast rather than three, but resolution is
        still bounded. The pooled seed-level SD of test accuracy is
        \num{0.284}~pp, rising to \num{0.43}~pp on the 3$\times$2000 MLPs,
        giving a bootstrap CI half-width near $\pm\num{0.35}$~pp at $n=5$
        and $\pm\num{0.26}$~pp at $n=7$. Against a $\pm\num{0.25}$~pp
        margin, a contrast below about \num{0.3}~pp cannot be resolved
        either way at those counts, which is why one is reported
        inconclusive. The ladder contrasts use \num{20} seeds or more.
  \item \textbf{Energy accounting is GPU-only.} NVML reports GPU package
        power; host CPU, DRAM and PCIe energy are not instrumented, so any
        variant that moves work off the accelerator is flattered. This
        matters most for the host-cache \MF{} strategy, whose true total
        cost is understated by an unknown amount --- we still report it as
        dominated, since it already loses on the instrumented axis. CO$_2$e
        estimates rest on a grid-intensity model we do not treat as a
        measurement and are reported only in the repository.
  \item \textbf{Hyperparameter search confound.} Each algorithm was tuned
        independently under an equal trial budget, so differences may partly
        reflect better hyperparameter discovery. The search cost is excluded
        from all reported figures; for a slow algorithm such as \FF{} it is
        substantial and belongs in any total-cost assessment.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} The contribution is evaluative --- the framework,
        the protocols, the ladder and the measurements. The algorithms are
        previously
        published~\cite{hinton2022forward,zhao2023cafo,gong2025mono}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented a hardware-validated benchmarking study of three forward-only,
BP-free training algorithms --- \FF{}, \CaFo{} and \MF{} --- against fairly
tuned \BP{} baselines, under one fairness framework, on MLP and CNN
architectures across four vision benchmarks.

On its native MLP architectures \MF{} matched or surpassed \BP{} accuracy in
every configuration, by \numrange{0.01}{5.04}~pp, and held up to \num{27}\%
less per-process device memory. It was not cheaper to train. Under a stopping
rule harmonised across algorithms, \MF{} is \num{4.0}$\times$ slower and draws
\num{3.2}$\times$ the energy of \BP{} on CIFAR-10; the \num{41}\% energy
saving reported in the accepted version is reproducible only under the
submitted protocol, in which each algorithm was allowed a different amount of
training before it could stop, and its sign reverses once that is equalised.
\FF{} and \CaFo{} are more expensive still on every axis but memory. An
ablation ladder attributes the increase almost entirely to gradient locality,
worth \numrange{228}{272}~pp of \BP{}'s energy, and shows that a
deep-supervision \BP{} control reproduces \MF{}'s accuracy on the MNIST-scale
tasks but falls more than \num{5}~pp short of it on CIFAR.

These results are specific to small architectures and benchmarks on a single
GPU, and their efficiency half is conditional on a platform where nothing we
measured exceeded ${\approx}\SI{38}{\percent}$ mean GPU utilisation. Within
that scope the work provides a reproducible framework, an attribution of cost
to a specific architectural property rather than to an algorithm as a whole,
and a corrected empirical basis --- including corrections to our own earlier
claims --- for evaluating BP-free methods.
